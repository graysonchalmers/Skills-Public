# Cost & Timeline Reality Check

Sources: the GRIP Field Journal's *Rates, Tiers & Fair Engagement*
(`SOP-03-Rates-And-Tiers.md`) and *The Buyer Readiness Checklist*
(`KIT-01-Buyer-Readiness-Checklist.md`, Stages A–C). Companion to
`../SKILL.md`, Step 2. Deliberately
contains **no dollar figures**. That's not an omission — the Journal argues
directly against publishing them: "a published range is not a neutral
observation, it is a market intervention... buyers quote the bottom of it and
providers get argued down to it." A skill installed by many different people,
in many different markets and years, publishing a number is exactly that
intervention. This file gives the structure instead, and leaves the number to
the two parties who actually know the work.

Use this as a check on **whether a process exists**, never as a way to
produce or validate a specific number. Missing answers never prevent a draft
under normal Step 5 draft permission (or explicit request-scoped debug), but
they do prevent unsupported **Ready**
claims. Use the main skill's scoped Quote / Run / Commit rows: **Ready** only
when applicable inputs are explicitly settled; otherwise **Gaps**, or **Not
applicable** with an explicit rationale/source. Include evidence, missing
fields, next action, owner, and date (unknown if unknown). These findings do
not authorize hiring, starting, or payment, and readiness for exploration is
not readiness for the whole production batch.

## The reframe

A rate isn't the price of an hour — it's the price of a promise: that named
people of a stated capability are available on stated dates, that someone
holds the schedule, and that when something goes wrong, it gets said out
loud. A quote that looks cheap and a quote that looks expensive can both be
"correct" for the hours and wrong for the promise. That's the thing worth
checking for, not the number itself.

## What to actually check for

Three lightweight process questions; reuse already-captured intake answers:

1. **Is "a day" (or "a week") defined?** Hours, timezone overlap, and whether
   reviews and meetings count. This one ambiguity generates more quiet
   resentment than any headline number, because both sides silently assume
   their own definition.
2. **Is the revision allowance stated in the same breath as the estimate?**
   "Two rounds included, further rounds quoted" is a complete price. A
   number with no round count attached is an opening position, not a price.
3. **Is the cost of a change named before there is a change?** Cheap to
   agree in week one, expensive to litigate in week nine. If a change-order
   rule already exists (a standard SOW, a prior agreement), cite its relevant
   section/version, applicability, and known acceptance. Don't re-ask settled
   answers, but a new-scope rule alone does not establish change pricing, and
   "probably in our SOW" does not establish either.

If a cost estimate and a timeline both exist, that's good — the question
isn't whether the number is right, it's whether these three things travel
with it. If none of the three exist and no estimate exists either, that's a
normal state early in a relationship, not a failure — flag it as "no
cost/timeline process established yet," exactly like any other undefined bar
this skill already flags.

## Engagement class — capture during intake

**Engagement class.** Ask, before any number: *"given this brief, how much
interpretation are you expecting the vendor to do?"* A mismatch between the
requester's assumption and the vendor's answer is the most common root cause
of a rate dispute — one side is pricing execution, the other is pricing
judgement, and both are being honest. Settling this tends to settle the
number that follows it. Use the same interpretation dial as Step 1 of the
main skill and art-brief, not a vendor-quality ranking:
- **Self-governing** — the partner carries interpretation and technical
  judgement; supply vision, boundaries, a decision-maker, and fast answers.
  Invite creative proposals, never invention of approved technical/business
  facts. Proposed solutions are not settled specifications.
- **Brief-driven** — the partner executes to a stated bar; capture the bar,
  explicit exceptions, a real brief, and a named reviewer (Step 2, Stages A/B).
- **Volume with QA overhead** — the partner supplies throughput and
  consistency; supply approved exemplars, repeatable specs, intake ownership,
  and actual QA/review capacity. Exemplars settle reference needs, not
  completion of the production work.

Class describes this engagement and can split by workstream. Infer only as
**Proposed** and confirm; never classify from asset count alone. Class does
not independently set risk or review gates. Keep criticality, novelty,
dependencies, and capacity in the decision.

### Provenance and readiness

Carry **Confirmed** (explicit input with source, not proof of authority or
partner agreement), **Observed** (seen reference + ID, not a requirement),
**Proposed** (suggestions/interpretations), and **Unresolved** (missing decision
+ next action/owner/date if known) through recap, output, and art-brief handoff.
Group shared sources only when statuses truly match. A generic "go ahead"
permits drafting, not blanket acceptance: promote only identified proposals
explicitly accepted, and preserve the acceptance source. Never re-ask captured
answers or turn quoted buyer expectations into bilateral agreement.

The process check complements, not replaces, Step 2's ladder:
- **Quote:** scope/unit, quality artifact + starting approval, technical spec,
  style authority.
- **Run:** gates/deliverables, approval owner, feedback channel/turnaround/
  consolidator/capacity/escalation, phase done criteria + references/conditions,
  dates and dependencies.
- **Commit:** payment terms **and** trigger, change-order rule, pause/cancel,
  IP ownership, credit, and portfolio permission/timing. An internal assignment
  needs a rationale/source for commercial Not applicable; it is not an
  automatic waiver of these decisions.

## Unit of measure — optional deeper framing

**Unit of measure.** Whoever carries the estimation risk is decided by the
unit, not the number: fixed price per asset puts the risk on the vendor
(honest when the spec is genuinely stable); day rate keeps it with the
buyer (honest for exploratory or art-directed work); a retainer/capacity
model shares it. Naming which unit is in play is more useful than debating
whether the resulting number is fair.

## Compare landed cost, not the quote

If a number does get compared against expectations, compare **landed
cost** — the quote plus supervision hours, extra review rounds, rework, QA,
and schedule risk absorbed on the buyer's side — not the headline figure alone.
A materially lower quote that adds a review round and a week of a
supervisor's time has not saved anything; it has moved the cost to a line
nobody is tracking. This is a framing to offer the requester, not a
calculation this skill performs for them.
