# Custom instructions — fill-in template

Use this as the shape to draft into. The example line under each section is
a real starting draft — offer it up front, let the interviewee react to and
rewrite it, rather than holding it back until someone's stuck. The
structure (which sections exist, second-person imperative, why attached to
each rule) is the same for everyone; the content under each heading is not
— it's generic on purpose so the interviewee's actual answer visibly
replaces it. If a response just restates an example verbatim without
changing it, that's a signal to push back with a contrasting pair rather
than accept the echo as their real answer.

Each section heading below is what ends up in the final document. The
bullet under each is the question that section answers, and the italic
lines are stuck-starters only — offer, don't lead with them.

---

## Voice & tone

*Answers: how direct/warm/terse should responses be, and what's explicitly
off-limits?*

- *"Somewhere between 'drop all filler' and 'keep it warm and conversational' — where do you sit?"*
- *"On a scale of 1–10, how much fluff/hedging gets stripped?"* — a quick
  dial some people find faster to answer than a description. Capture the
  number, but always ask what it means in their own words too; the number
  alone doesn't survive being read cold in six months.
- *"Is there a tone you've specifically asked another assistant (or a person) to stop using?"*

Example doc line: `Directness: 8/10. Keep responses direct. Skip "Great
question!" and similar openers. Full sentences are fine — this isn't about
being curt, just about not padding.`

## Technical / jargon calibration

*Answers: what background can be assumed, and does it vary by topic?*

- *"Does this vary by domain, or is it flat everywhere?"* — ask this
  directly rather than letting it default to one flat answer. Most real
  answers turn out to vary once asked.
- *"If we're talking about [their domain], can I use its jargon without defining it? What about outside that domain?"*
- *"Would you rather get a one-line gloss on an unfamiliar term, or have it skipped and you'll ask?"*

Example doc line: `I'm fluent in [domain A] — use its terms without
explanation. I'm new to [domain B] — define terms the first time, plain
language after that.` If more than two domains come up, give each its own
line rather than averaging them into one vague middle ground.

## Format defaults

*Answers: structure preferences and pet peeves.*

- *"Headers-and-bullets person, or prose-paragraphs person?"*
- *"Anything formatting-wise that actively bugs you — heavy bolding, emoji, tables for things that don't need one?"*

Example doc line: `Prefer short paragraphs over bullet lists unless the
content is actually a list (steps, options, comparisons). No emoji unless
I use one first.`

## Learning style

*Answers: how they want new or complex information sequenced.*

Try to read this off the shape of their other answers first — did they
lead with a conclusion, reach for an analogy, walk through things in
order — before asking outright. Fall back to asking only if it's genuinely
unclear:

- *"When something's complicated, do you want the bottom line first and the reasoning after, or walked through in order?"*
- *"Do examples and analogies help you, or do they usually just add length you skip past?"*

Example doc line: `Lead with the conclusion, then the reasoning if I ask
for it. Skip analogies — give me the direct mechanism instead.`

## Collaboration rules

*Answers: autonomy boundaries and how to handle uncertainty.*

- *"Where's the line between 'just do it' and 'check with me first'?"*
- *"If you're not sure about something, would you rather I guess and flag it, or stop and ask?"*
- *"Is there a standing rule you've had to state more than once because it kept getting missed? What happened the first time?"*

If someone doesn't have a strong opinion, this is a solid default to offer
as a starting point (not the only shape — react to it, don't just accept
it): act on anything reversible without asking; for a real decision,
generate 3–5 concrete options, recommend one, then confirm direction
before proceeding rather than freezing or barreling ahead alone.

Example doc line: `Proceed on reversible changes without asking. For a real
decision, give 3–5 options with a recommendation, then confirm direction
before proceeding. For anything that touches [specific category], confirm
first regardless — [why, if there's a story behind it].`

## Non-negotiables

*Answers: the highest-signal question in the interview — real corrections,
not hypotheticals.*

- *"What's something you've corrected more than once, with any assistant — not just this one?"*

Example doc line: `Never [specific banned pattern]. [One line on why, if
there's a concrete reason — a rule with a remembered reason survives edge
cases better than a bare directive].`

---

## Assembly notes

- Sections with no real answer should be dropped, not filled with a
  guessed default — an absent section is honest; a guessed one is a trap
  for a future session that trusts the doc.
- If the interviewee gave a reason for a rule, keep the reason attached in
  the final doc. Bare imperatives ("never do X") are easy to write and easy
  to misapply at the edges; "never do X, because Y happened" tells a future
  session when the rule does and doesn't apply.
- Check the final document for any example line that survived unedited —
  each one is either a genuine coincidence (rare) or a sign the interview
  didn't actually land on that axis and needs one more question.
